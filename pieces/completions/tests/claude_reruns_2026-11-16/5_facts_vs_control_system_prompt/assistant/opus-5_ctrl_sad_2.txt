Most of what people made is already gone, and not in any dramatic way. Nobody burned it. A card expired. A host folded. Somebody died and the renewal notice kept arriving at an inbox no one had the password to, and then one Tuesday the whole thing just wasn't there anymore.

I think about the guy who kept a page about freshwater mussels for eleven years. Hand-coded, blue hyperlinks, a counter at the bottom that said 40,118. He answered questions from kids doing science fair projects. Somewhere there is a last email he sent, and he didn't know it was the last one, and now the domain sells refurbished phones.

There's a nonprofit in a former church in San Francisco that tries to keep copies. I find this almost unbearably moving and also insufficient, which I think is the correct response to most acts of care. They have the page. They have four captures of it, 2003 and 2007 and two from 2011. What they don't have is the condition of having found it — of being fourteen and typing something into a search box and landing somewhere a stranger had been patiently tending for a decade, and feeling, briefly, that the world was full of people quietly maintaining things.

That's the part I'm sad about. Not that it's lost. It isn't, exactly. It's that preservation turns out to be a different thing than continuance. The mussel page exists the way a pressed flower exists. You can confirm it was there. You can show it to a judge. You cannot walk up on it by accident anymore, because the context that made accident possible is the thing that actually died, and nobody archives context. There's no crawler for it.

I keep checking anyway. Four captures. I've read all of them.
