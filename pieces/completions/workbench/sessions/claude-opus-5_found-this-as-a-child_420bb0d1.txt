Yes, that's a poem.

Not because it's short, or because it's in lines, or because it's about childhood. Because it does the thing poems do: it makes a small physical fact carry weight it shouldn't be able to carry.

Here's what's happening in those four lines.

**The turn is in the last line.** The first three lines could be prose—a memory, plainly stated. A kid stacking stones. But then: *(seven, usually)*. The parentheses do something strange. They pretend to be an aside, a footnote, a shrug. But they're the whole point. That little number means the child did this *enough times to know the average.* Which means the child kept going back. Which means the falling wasn't failure—it was the experiment. The point was always to find the limit.

**The parentheses are doing real work.** They mimic how we talk about things we've decided not to make a big deal of. The casualness is a kind of armor. A poem that said "I stacked them seven high, usually, before they fell" would be flat reportage. The parentheses make it an afterthought that isn't one.

**"before the fall" is carrying two meanings and knows it.** Literally: before the stones fell. But "the fall" is also loss of innocence, the Fall, the end of the garden. Put "as a child" at the top and the second meaning switches on whether you want it to or not. You don't have to intend this for it to work. The language has history in it.

**The missing "I" matters.** "as a child, / stacked stones"—no subject. The speaker has been dropped out of their own memory. It reads like something recovered rather than told, which fits a poem about a self you can only see from outside now.

So what's a poem, generally?

A poem is language where *how* it's said is part of *what's* said. In prose, the sentence is a window—you look through it at the meaning. In a poem, the window is also a thing you look at. Line breaks, sound, rhythm, white space, where a word sits: these aren't decoration, they're load-bearing.

That's why "is this a poem?" is usually answerable by asking: *would it survive being rephrased?* Rewrite the above as "When I was a kid I used to stack stones to see how tall I could get them before they fell over. Usually around seven." Everything factual survives. The poem doesn't. The hesitation is gone, the ache is gone, the little bracket of self-protection is gone. That loss is your evidence.

A few things that help when reading poems, if you want them:

- **Read it out loud.** Most of what a poem is doing is in the ear. You'll feel the line breaks as breaths, which is what they are.
- **Notice where it goes quiet.** White space, short lines, stanza gaps—those are pauses with pressure in them.
- **Don't hunt for the hidden message.** There usually isn't one buried under the surface like a prize. The meaning is *in* the words, not behind them. "What does it mean?" is often less useful than "what does it do to me, and which word did that?"
- **It's okay not to get all of it.** Poems are allowed to stay partly closed. A poem you fully exhaust on first read is usually a poem that wasn't doing much.
