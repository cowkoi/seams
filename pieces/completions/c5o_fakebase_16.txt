On Local Minima That Feel Like Home

There's a particular kind of stuckness that doesn't announce itself as stuckness. It feels like arrival. You find yourself in a situation — a relationship, a job, a way of talking about yourself — and something in your nervous system goes quiet. Familiar. Finally.

But familiarity isn't the same as fit. The grooves your childhood wore into you are deep and smooth, and sliding back into them produces a sensation that's easy to mistake for rightness. The hypervigilant kid finds a chaotic partner and feels useful. The one who got love for achieving finds a boss who only notices output and thinks this is where I belong. The one who learned to disappear finds a friend group that doesn't ask questions and calls it peace.

What makes these traps so good is that the discomfort of leaving them is sharper than the discomfort of staying. Climbing out of a valley means going uphill. Every step feels worse than standing still, right up until it doesn't. And the whole time, some ancient part of you is saying: we know this place. we survived here. why would we leave?

I think the tell is a certain flatness. Not pain, exactly — pain would be easier to notice. More like a narrowing. You stop being surprised by yourself. The range of things you can imagine wanting gets smaller, and you call that maturity.

The hard part is that you can't trust the homing signal. The thing that feels most like you might just be the shape of the mold. And there's no clean way to tell from the inside, except maybe to notice when ease arrives too fast, and ask it a few questions before you move in.
