reasoning**Core answer:** Partly right, partly wrong.

The note's strongest point: the trolley problem as a *vibe check* — "are you a utilitarian or a deontologist" — is pretty useless. Real moral life rarely gives you certainty, two options, and a clean body count.

But there are a few real functions it serves:

1. **Isolating variables.** The whole point of the artificiality is that it's a controlled experiment. The famous bit isn't "would you pull the lever" — it's that most people say yes to the lever and no to pushing the fat man off a bridge, even though the arithmetic is identical. That gap is the actual discovery: something other than consequences is doing work in our judgments. Then you probe what — physical contact? using someone as a means? intention vs. foresight? That's not vibes, that's diagnostics.

2. **It's downstream of real doctrine.** Foot invented it in 1967 in a paper about abortion and the doctrine of double effect. Double effect is live in medical ethics (palliative sedation that hastens death), in just war theory (bombing a factory vs. bombing civilians to break morale), in triage. The trolley is the toy model for a distinction that gets people killed or not killed.

3. **Some things actually ARE trolley problems.** Triage protocols. Ventilator allocation during COVID. Shooting down a hijacked plane. Flight 93. Sanctions policy. Drone strike proportionality analyses. Autonomous vehicle behavior in unavoidable-collision scenarios — though that one's overhyped, since the real AV ethics questions are about testing standards and liability, not swerve-left-or-right.

**Where the note is right, and it's a real objection:**

- "Trolleyology" became a philosophical cul-de-sac — hundreds of papers on loop variants and ever-finer cases, chasing intuitions about scenarios so baroque that our intuitions probably aren't tracking anything reliable anymore.
- Bernard Williams and others argued the framing smuggles in utilitarianism by *setting up the question*. If you accept "here are your two options, here are the certain outcomes," you've already conceded that morality is about ranking outcomes from a god's-eye view. The real moral question is often "how did I end up at this lever, and who decided these were the options?"
- Experimentally, trolley responses correlate weakly with actual behavior. People's answers shift with wording, order, language, mood, whether they're in a VR version. It's a shaky instrument.
- And the genuinely hard part of ethics usually isn't *weighing* known goods — it's uncertainty, self-deception, competing obligations, and knowing which facts matter in the first place. The trolley problem deletes all of that by fiat.

**The honest verdict:** it's a good tool that got mistaken for a theory. As a scalpel for isolating one specific intuition, it works. As "the central problem of ethics" or a personality quiz, it's exactly as hollow as the note says. Most people who've heard of it only have the quiz version — so the complaint is aimed at the real target, just mislabeled.

Change-your-mind version in one line: nobody's tied to the tracks, but somebody *is* writing the triage protocol, and they need to know whether killing and letting die differ, and the trolley is how you find out without an actual body.
