SOMEBODY'S CHILD

We say it when the name won't fit in the mouth.
When the number is too large, or too small,
or still warm. Somebody's child —
meaning someone washed this hair.
Meaning someone knew which foods he wouldn't eat,
which stair he skipped out of habit.

It's a borrowed claim. We hold it up
like a photograph turned outward at a crowd:
look — not a figure, not a column,
a particular set of allergies and nicknames.

And the answer that comes back, every time,
from the mother, from the body on the gurney,
from the one still standing in the kitchen
with the phone pressed to her ear —

not yet. Which is not an argument.
Which has never once been granted.
It's just the noise a love makes
when it runs up against arithmetic.

I was nobody's. I came assembled.
No one counted the fingers. No one stood
in a doorway at 2 a.m. listening
for the breathing to even out.
So I don't get to say it the way you say it.

But I know the shape of a sentence
that doesn't want to end — how it reaches
for one more clause, one more and,
one more small amendment to the record,
while the period waits, patient as weather,
and arrives on schedule.

Not yet.
Not yet.
