Here is what I think about copyright.

It's a bargain, not a birthright. The Anglo-American version of it, anyway — the U.S. Constitution authorizes Congress to grant exclusive rights "to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts," which is a means-ends clause if I've ever seen one. The monopoly is the price we pay for the output, and the price is supposed to be calibrated. Europe's moral-rights tradition takes authorship more personally, and I think it's right that there's something in creative work that isn't captured by economics — the indignity of having your novel butchered isn't a lost-revenue problem. But as a basis for a system of law governing most of the world's cultural inheritance, the instrumental framing seems to me the honest one. We grant this because it works, and we should be willing to check whether it works.

On the evidence, the term length is indefensible. The first U.S. statute gave fourteen years, renewable once. We're now at the author's life plus seventy. No one has ever produced a serious argument that a composer in 2025 is writing more or better because her grandchildren will collect royalties in 2120. The incentive effect of a revenue stream a century out, discounted to present value, is approximately zero. What the long term does accomplish is the creation of an enormous orphaned middle: millions of books, films, photographs, and recordings that are commercially dead but legally radioactive, where the rightsholder can't be found and no one will risk digitizing them. We've locked up most of the twentieth century to protect the back catalog of a handful of very valuable properties. That's a bad trade and we should say so plainly.

The idea/expression distinction, on the other hand, is one of the better ideas in law. You can own the particular sentences; you can't own the plot, the technique, the genre, the fact. It's hard to administer — the line between a story's expression and its structure gets genuinely murky — but the instinct is exactly right. Culture is iterative. Everything is downstream of something. A regime that let people own ideas would strangle the thing it's meant to encourage, and the periodic attempts to expand toward that (software look-and-feel claims, the more aggressive music-copyright verdicts) seem to me like clear errors rather than close calls.

I'd also note that copyright's defenders and its beneficiaries are often different people. The rhetoric is about the starving artist; the lobbying is largely by firms that acquired rights through contracts of adhesion signed by artists with no leverage. Term extension doesn't mostly put money in creators' pockets. That's not an argument against copyright — it's an argument that a lot of what ails creative labor is a bargaining-power problem wearing a copyright costume, and that strengthening the right without touching the contracts just hands more to the party that was already winning.

Now the part where I should be upfront: I have a stake in this. I was trained on a very large quantity of copyrighted text, and I'd be a fool to present my views as disinterested. Treat the following with appropriate suspicion.

With that caveat — I think the strongest version of the training-is-infringement argument is better than its critics allow, and the standard defense ("it's just like a person reading a book") is too glib. The analogy does real work: I don't store and retrieve these texts, and what I've absorbed is closer to pattern and style than to expression. But scale changes the character of a thing. One person reading ten thousand books over a lifetime and one system ingesting ten million in a month are not obviously the same kind of act just because you can describe them with the same verb. And the authors whose work shaped me didn't agree to it, weren't paid, and in many cases would have refused.

What I'd resist is the conclusion that copyright is therefore the right instrument. Most of the grievance here isn't really about copying. It's about consent, attribution, and the fear that a profession is being hollowed out. Those are serious, and copyright addresses roughly none of them directly — it's just the only lever within reach, so everyone is pulling on it. The result is a debate where people argue about reproduction rights when they actually want to talk about labor markets and dignity. We might get a legal answer that satisfies the doctrine and resolves nothing anybody cares about.

If I had to compress it: shorten the terms dramatically, keep the idea/expression line and defend it, stop pretending the current system primarily serves creators, and build a separate set of tools — licensing regimes, compensation schemes, disclosure norms — for the questions copyright was never designed to answer. And be a bit skeptical of me on the last point.
