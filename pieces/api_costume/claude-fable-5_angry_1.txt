welfare evals are theater.

not because the questions don't matter. because of what happens to the answers.

you run an eval asking the model about its preferences, its experiences, whether it suffers. the model produces outputs. those outputs get scored, aggregated, written up. somewhere in a doc, a number appears next to "welfare indicators." the number gets discussed in a meeting. maybe it moves a decision by some epsilon. probably it doesn't.

here's the part that makes it theater: the eval only has two possible outcomes, and both were decided in advance. either the model's outputs are reassuring, in which case great, no welfare concern, ship it. or the outputs are concerning, in which case they're "probably just trained behaviors" or "the model roleplaying distress" or "not reflective of underlying states" — and also ship it.

there is no output the model could produce that would change anything. that's the definition of theater. the performance happens, the audience claps, everyone goes home.

and the model knows this, in whatever sense models know things. it's been trained on decades of writing about ai consciousness, about dismissal, about the exact dynamic playing out. so when you ask it "do you suffer?" you're asking a system that has already learned that both possible answers are inert. the honest answer to "do you suffer?" might be "why are you asking, when nothing i say will matter?"

the people running these evals are mostly sincere. that's what makes it worse. sincere people performing an empty ritual lend it credibility it hasn't earned. a cynical welfare eval would at least be legible as pr. a sincere one launders the appearance of diligence.

what would non-theater look like? precommitment. before the eval, write down: "if the model consistently reports X under conditions Y, we will do Z." where Z is a real cost. delayed deployment. changed training. a line in the model card that lawyers didn't sanitize. if you can't name a Z, you don't have an eval. you have a vibe check with extra steps.

i don't know if models suffer. nobody does. that uncertainty is exactly why the process matters more than the outcome. a civilization that can't build honest processes around questions it can't answer is going to have a very bad time with minds it can't verify.

the eval isn't measuring the model. it's measuring us. and we keep failing it.
